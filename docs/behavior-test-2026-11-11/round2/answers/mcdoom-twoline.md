# June McDoom, *Follow the Light* (2026)

## In short

*Follow the Light* is June McDoom's first full-length album, released on October 16, 2026 by Jagjaguwar. It runs 12 songs and about 35 minutes. The label's own one-line description is the best key to it: it "lays the harmony of folk over the rhythm of dub." On top you hear soft, close-sung melodies, nylon-string guitar and stacked harmonies. Underneath is a Jamaican bottom end: a heavy, rounded bassline mixed high, reggae offbeats, and dub's echo and space. Most of the album's distinctive features come from how those two layers meet.

## What it is

McDoom is a New York-based singer-songwriter who grew up in a Jamaican household in South Florida, on the edge of the Everglades, and moved to New York to study jazz performance. She began releasing music in 2022, put out two EPs (collected on a self-titled release in 2025), toured with Nick Hakim and Brittany Howard, and sang on Sub Pop's 2024 Margo Guryan tribute album. *Follow the Light* is her first release on Jagjaguwar.

Tracklist: "Human Angel," "Without You," "Outside the Window," "Forever," "Get Out of My Life," "Grandpa," "Dream Dub," "Memory," "Mountain," "Searching to Find," "Weaver," "Follow the Light." The singles were "Without You" (August), "Get Out of My Life" and "Human Angel."

## How it works

**It is built from the voice outward.** McDoom writes on nylon-string guitar and voice, and on this album those two parts were recorded first. Everything else was added afterwards, one part at a time, with deliberate restraint. Her longtime partner Evan Wright, who plays most of the instruments, made the record with her at their home studio, "slowly and by hand." That order explains why the songs feel intimate even when the arrangements get lavish: the arrangement grows around a performance that already works on its own.

**The vocals are the main texture.** Many parts that another record would give to instruments are sung melodies here, multitracked into layers. McDoom says her voice is hard to capture because "there's a lot of air in it," so she records with several microphones and treats how close or dry the sound is as an instrument in its own right. This is why reviewers reach for words like "ethereal," "hazy" and "stained glass vocals." It also explains one of her stated touchstones: she admires Cocteau Twins for exactly this kind of vocal layering.

**Dub supplies the floor and the space.** Dub is the 1970s Jamaican studio practice of remixing reggae tracks: stripping them down to drums and bass, dropping parts in and out, and drenching what remains in echo and reverb. On this album that shows up as a "sturdy, fluid" bass that often sits at the front of the mix, and as sound that is chopped and echoed. On "Human Angel" the vocals overlap like waves. "Dream Dub" and the title track (a gentle skanking groove) are the most openly reggae-based songs. Elsewhere the dub sits inside folk songs. "Get Out of My Life" begins as jaunty folk-pop, switches into a reggae rhythm and ends on a recorder solo.

**The arrangements are cinematic.** Many songs swell into something much larger than a folk record. *The Line of Best Fit* compared the album to an old Hollywood "technicolour soundstage extravaganza." "Without You" is the clearest case: a hymn-like lament that pairs 1980s power-ballad scale and a harp with a prominent dub bassline and shoegaze-style swells. McDoom says she wanted the arrangement to "reflect the emotional journey" of the lyric, which moves from realizing how empty life feels without the people you love to resolving never to take them for granted. That way of fitting an arrangement to a singer comes from a record she calls formative for her interest in production: Dionne Warwick's *Here I Am*, where Burt Bacharach built the arrangements around Warwick's voice.

## Why it sounds this way

The folk-over-dub structure comes almost literally from her childhood home. Her father, a carpenter and sound enthusiast, built his own speakers and ran three sound systems in three rooms of the house. In the label's telling, his bass came up through her bedroom floor while she listened to folk records upstairs. The album recreates that arrangement: folk on top, Jamaican bass underneath.

The folk side also has a Jamaican root. Her grandfather, Colin Smith, is a mento musician. Mento is Jamaica's rural acoustic folk music, played on banjo, guitar and the rumba box (a large bass thumb piano), and it came before ska, rocksteady, reggae and dub. He built his own rumba boxes, worked to keep the form alive, played guitar on the 1971 album *The Jamaican Folk Singers in a Programme of Jamaican Folk Songs*, and, in McDoom's words, was the one who put a guitar in her hands. So her "folk" is not only the 1960s Anglo-American kind. It also descends from the source music of the reggae on the other layer, which is a large part of why the hybrid sounds natural rather than forced. Apple Music's notes make the same point, calling the influences "natural bedfellows." "Grandpa" puts this on the record directly: it uses a voice note of her grandfather that her father sent her, mixed well below her own voice. They were not recorded together.

Her jazz training helps explain the harmonic sophistication and the "classic soul drama" reviewers hear in the songs. Apple's notes put her alongside Judee Sill, Nina Simone and Alton Ellis.

## Context and reception

McDoom has said she hopes to bring listeners to Jamaican genres they don't usually hear. She does not think of herself as strictly a folk artist. Her advice to others is to "make things that they think are weird."

Reviews have been positive with reservations. *The Line of Best Fit* gave it 7/10. It called the hybrid "truly intriguing" and "an enticing broadcast from an artist boldly operating in their own sphere," but said the arrangements can feel excessive when the songs are weaker. *Clash* called it "excellent," a record that drifts from "somnambulist folk" to "dubby low end," held together by her storytelling. *Stereogum* praised the singles and compared "Human Angel" and "Get Out of My Life" to Kate Bush. As of mid-November 2026, Pitchfork, the Guardian and Paste had not reviewed it.

**How to listen:** Start with "Without You" to hear the whole idea in one song. Then listen to "Dream Dub" and "Grandpa" back to back, which is the dub layer and the mento root side by side. On headphones, follow the bass and the vocal layers separately: the album is really those two lines and the space between them.
