# June McDoom, *Follow the Light* (Jagjaguwar, October 16, 2026)

*I haven't heard the record. What follows comes from McDoom's own interviews, the label's description and the reviews listed at the end.*

## What it is

*Follow the Light* is June McDoom's first full-length album, released on Jagjaguwar after a few years of self-released EPs. It has 12 songs: "Human Angel," "Without You," "Outside the Window," "Forever," "Get Out of My Life," "Grandpa," "Dream Dub," "Memory," "Mountain," "Searching to Find," "Weaver" and "Follow the Light." It usually gets filed as indie-folk, and McDoom pushes back on that. A one-line summary: soft, close-up folk songs sung over a Jamaican low end, recorded with the layered care of a 1960s pop production.

That mix makes sense once you know where she comes from. She grew up in a Jamaican family near the Florida Everglades. Her father was a carpenter who built his own speakers and ran sound systems at home. Her grandfather, Colin Smith, was a respected mento musician who played banjo and guitar, built rumba boxes, and gave her a guitar. She later moved to New York to study jazz performance, and she has toured with Nick Hakim and Brittany Howard. The album pulls those three things together: the father's sound system, the grandfather's acoustic tradition, and a trained ear for harmony.

## How it was made

McDoom writes each song on nylon-string guitar and voice and leaves everything else for later. Her partner and long-time collaborator Evan Wright, a pianist, plays most of the other instruments. Many of his parts start as vocal melodies she sings to him, which he then works out on piano. Each part is multitracked. Because her voice is very airy, she says no single microphone captures it, so she records with several and fits the takes together "like a puzzle." The label describes the process as slow, done by hand on analogue equipment, with lean arrangements built up part by part.

That process explains the record's two main traits. First, it is crowded with voices and still sounds intimate, because the extra layers are mostly her own voice rather than a band. Second, critics hear it as a whole sound world rather than a set of songs with arrangements added afterwards. McDoom says she treats the sound of the recording as an instrument in its own right.

## The three things that carry it

### 1. The bass and the echo come from dub

The feature every reviewer notices first is the low end. The Line of Best Fit calls the basslines "sturdy, fluid" and "front and centre," and describes sound being chopped and twisted as if in an echo chamber. Clash describes the album moving from sleepwalking folk to "dubby low end." Even the tender lead single "Without You" puts a dub bassline and live drums under the harp and the swells.

That comes from dub, a style of Jamaican record-making from the 1970s. Engineers like King Tubby and Lee "Scratch" Perry took a finished reggae track, pulled the vocal out, pushed bass and drums to the front, and dropped parts in and out through heavy echo and reverb. The mixing desk itself became the instrument. McDoom heard this at home through her father's sound-system sets. It explains the album's gravity: a quiet acoustic song gets a deep, moving floor instead of a strummed one. It also explains why the echo sounds structural rather than decorative. "Dream Dub" and the gently skanking title track (skank is the reggae offbeat strum) make the link most obvious. On "Get Out of My Life," Stereogum hears a folk-pop song turn into a reggae rhythm and a recorder solo near the end.

### 2. The folk underneath is Jamaican as well as Anglo-American

"Folk" usually suggests a British or American singer-songwriter. McDoom has two folk sources, and one of them is her family's. Mento is Jamaica's older acoustic music, from before ska. It is played on banjo, acoustic guitar, hand drums and a rumba box (a large thumb piano that you sit on, which supplies the bass). Its lyrics are wry songs about everyday life. It was the first Jamaican music ever recorded commercially, and ska and reggae grew out of it. Her grandfather spent his life keeping it going, and he plays guitar on a 1971 record by the Jamaican Folk Singers that she cites as a foundation.

Knowing this changes how the album hangs together. The reggae and the folk aren't two genres she bolted together. They are the same family line, about sixty years apart, and the album places them side by side. It does so literally: her grandfather appears on one track through a voice note her father forwarded from Florida. His very deep voice sits far below hers, and she points out that it sounds as if they recorded together when they didn't. The song titled "Grandpa" is the likely one, though I haven't confirmed that. The label sums up the album's theme as inheritance: all the earlier versions of ourselves that past generations shaped, and the idea that a future self is "another ancestor waiting to be met." That reads as the best clue to the title, though she hasn't explained it directly.

The other folk source is the British folk-revival sound, with Pentangle named among the album's reference points. Pentangle mixed traditional song with jazz rhythm and close harmony. That fits a singer trained in jazz who is also, by her own description, "nerdy" about harmony learned from folk records.

### 3. The layered voices and the opulence come from 1960s and 1980s production

The third strand, and the one critics divide over, is how lavish it sounds. McDoom says Dionne Warwick's *Here I Am* first got her interested in recording and producing. What drew her was how Burt Bacharach placed each instrument around Warwick's voice. Bacharach built his arrangements around the singer, with each instrument given its own spot and entrance. That is a good way to hear Wright's harp, piano and swells: they are placed around her voice, not stacked behind it. The label also names Phil Spector's Wall of Sound and Minnie Riperton, a soul singer known for a very high, light voice set in lush orchestral arrangements.

For vocal texture she points to the Cocteau Twins, especially *Milk & Kisses* and *Victorialand*. *Victorialand* is the band's sparsest record, mostly guitar and Elizabeth Fraser's layered voice, with drums and bass largely removed. That is the model for songs where layered vocals do the work of a band. "Human Angel" opens the album with voices overlapping "like waves," in The Line of Best Fit's phrase. While recording she also discovered Popul Vuh's *Hosianna Mantra*, a 1970s album of electric guitar with oboe and violin under an almost devotional soprano. It became a major reference, which helps explain the hymn-like feel critics hear in "Without You."

Two reviewers compare her to Kate Bush. Stereogum hears it in the drums of "Get Out of My Life" and in the "regal" sophisti-pop of "Human Angel." She hasn't named Bush herself, so take that as a resemblance.

## The songs and what they're about

The lyrics get less attention than the sound, but the singles show the emotional range. "Without You" is about realizing "how meaningless life can feel without the person/people you love." McDoom says she wanted the arrangement to follow that realization and to end by celebrating a commitment not to take that person for granted, since it is "never too late to change how you've been." The Line of Best Fit hears it as an "80s power ballad" with harp and dub bass, delivered like a hymn. "Get Out of My Life" goes the other way: a "defiant yet soft-focus" song about shaking off harmful influences. So the record spans devotion and release, and the family thread runs underneath both.

## Reception and context

Coverage is warm but not ecstatic. The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10. It called the hybrid "startling" at its best and "an enticing broadcast from an artist boldly operating in their own sphere." It also warned that on weaker songs the lushness turns cloying, "like gorging on candyfloss." Clash called it a "remarkable achievement" that sounds natural and unified. That split follows from how it was made: a record built from dozens of stacked, perfected parts carries the most weight when the song underneath is strong, and can feel overdone when it isn't.

She arrives in a crowded field of quiet, introspective songwriters on the folk side of indie. Most of them come from Anglo-American folk and dream-pop. What sets McDoom apart is the Jamaican material: the bass weight, the dub space and the mento lineage. In her interviews she connects that to a broader point. She dislikes genre labels, which she sees as especially limiting for women who play guitar, and she describes the album as an account of her own history rather than a style exercise.

**What's her own:** none of these influences fully accounts for the combination. It is a jazz-trained singer's close harmony, multiplied and arranged like Bacharach, over a dub floor her father played her, with her mento-playing grandfather's actual voice inside it. If you're listening for the first time, focus on the bass under the quietest songs and the stacked harmonies in "Human Angel." Those are the two halves of what the album is doing.
