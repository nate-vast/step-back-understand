# June McDoom, *Follow the Light* (Jagjaguwar, October 16, 2026)

*Follow the Light* sets folk songwriting on top of Jamaican low end. McDoom writes songs the way a folk singer does, alone on a nylon-string guitar. She then records them the way a 1960s pop producer would, building every part up from stacked vocal lines. Underneath it all she lays the music of her Jamaican family: dub bass and echo, reggae rhythm, and her grandfather's mento. Most of what is odd or distinctive about the record comes from those three layers sitting together. The quiet, dreamy surface belongs to the songs and the voice. The weight and sway come from the Jamaican music. The lushness comes from how carefully she builds and records each part.

(I haven't heard the album. What follows is drawn from McDoom's own statements and from critics who have.)

## What it is

This is McDoom's debut full-length: 12 songs in about 35 minutes. Before it she put out two EPs, and her early releases were collected on a self-titled compilation in 2025. She grew up in a Jamaican family in South Florida and moved to New York to study jazz performance. She began releasing music in 2022 and has toured with Nick Hakim and Brittany Howard. She made the album with her partner and longtime collaborator Evan Wright, a pianist who plays most of the instruments. The singles were "Without You," "Get Out of My Life" and "Human Angel."

Reviews have been warm. The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10 and called it "an enticing broadcast from an artist boldly operating in their own sphere." Clash called it excellent and said it moves "from somnambulist folk aspects to dubby low end." McDoom dislikes being filed as a folk singer ("I feel like I've been forced to be like, 'I'm a folk singer'"). The album makes that complaint easy to understand.

## Layer one: the songs and the voice

The songs start as folk songs. She writes on nylon-string guitar and voice, and she gets the song's basic shape settled before anything else. That is why the album still reads as songwriting rather than texture: each track has a melody and a story that would stand up with one guitar. Clash credits her "finely honed sense of storytelling" with holding the record together. The emotional core is plain. She describes "Without You" as being about "realizing how meaningless life can feel without the person/people you love." "Get Out of My Life" is about shedding bad energy and reaching for something brighter.

Her voice is soft and has, in her words, "a lot of air" in it. That affects the whole record. An airy voice doesn't punch through a dense mix, so the album is built around it rather than over it. She also records it through several microphones at once and blends them, because each mic catches a different part of the breath and tone.

## Layer two: building every part from the voice

This is the album's least obvious feature and the one that explains the most. McDoom calls herself "a super big part person." She multitracks each line, and she says most of the arrangement, bass included, begins as melodies she sings. Wright then fills those parts out on instruments once she has sung the top lines. So the counter-melodies, harmonies and even bass lines on the record are, at root, vocal lines. That is why critics keep landing on words like "lush," "opulent" and "stained-glass vocals." The Line of Best Fit points to the opener "Human Angel," where layered voices overlap like waves.

Two reference points she names explain where that habit comes from. She calls Dionne Warwick's *Here I Am* formative for her love of recording and producing. What she credits there is Burt Bacharach's way of setting "all the sounds around Dionne in the most special way." He wrote orchestral parts that frame a light voice instead of competing with it, which is the problem her own soft voice poses. She also names Cocteau Twins, whose records rest on Elizabeth Fraser's stacked, overlapping vocals. Her current favourite is *Milk & Kisses*, and she has cited *Victorialand*, their sparse, mostly drumless album. She says she loves their "melodies and the vocal layering." The haze and reverb on *Follow the Light*, which reviewers call shoegaze-like, sit closer to that tradition than to guitar-noise shoegaze.

## Layer three: Jamaican music underneath

This layer makes the record more than another dreamy folk album, and it's the one most listeners won't have the vocabulary for. McDoom has said she wants to introduce people to Jamaican music, because few people explore beyond reggae. Three styles matter here.

**Dub** is a Jamaican studio form from the late 1960s and 1970s. Producers such as King Tubby and Lee "Scratch" Perry took existing reggae tracks and remixed them. They pulled the vocals mostly out, pushed the drums and bass to the front, and soaked what remained in echo and reverb. On *Follow the Light* this shows up as heavy bass and echo under songs that would otherwise float. "Without You" pairs a harp with a prominent dub bass line. The Line of Best Fit likened it to an '80s power ballad, and its second half swells upward. "Dream Dub" wears the influence in its title. This is the main source of the album's physical weight. Dub puts the bass first, so a fragile vocal can drift above it without the song losing its footing.

**Reggae rhythm** shows up more openly in a few places. The title track is "gently skanking," meaning it uses reggae's offbeat chop. "Get Out of My Life" turns into a reggae groove near the end, followed by a recorder solo. McDoom has also cited her father's DJ sets as an influence, which fits this side of the record.

**Mento** is the deepest root. It is Jamaica's rural folk music, popular in the 1940s and 1950s, before ska and reggae grew out of it. It is played on acoustic guitar, banjo, hand drums and the rumba box, a big thumb piano you sit on that serves as the bass. Its lyrics are usually witty and topical. McDoom's grandfather is a mento musician. He is one of the guitarists on a Jamaican Folk Singers record she names among her foundations, and she grew up hearing him play its songs. He appears on "Grandpa" through a voice note her father sent from Florida, answering her vocals. The two were recorded separately, and his voice sits far below hers in the mix, yet it plays as a duet. That track is the album's thesis in miniature. Her folk singing and Jamaica's own folk music turn out to be relatives, not opposites. That family tie is why the folk and reggae elements fit together rather than sounding pasted on.

## Other things in the mix

Her jazz training probably shows in her phrasing and harmony, though she hasn't spelled out how. Apple Music's notes also tie her to Judee Sill, Nina Simone and rocksteady singer Alton Ellis. She has said that Popol Vuh's *Hosianna Mantra*, a hushed, devotional 1972 record, became "a big reference" while she was recording because of how "pure" it sounds. That points to the album's reverent, unhurried mood more than to any particular sound. Two Stereogum writers heard Kate Bush in the drums and theatrical pop of "Human Angel" and "Get Out of My Life." McDoom hasn't named Bush, so treat that as a resemblance. It does fit the off-kilter, busy drumming they describe.

## What to listen for, and the catch

The most rewarding way in is to listen downward. Notice how far below the voice the bass sits and how much echo surrounds it. Notice that the harmonies and counter-lines are voices. Notice when a folk song quietly starts to skank. "Without You," "Dream Dub," "Grandpa" and the title track show the blend most clearly.

The Line of Best Fit names the record's limitation: because every sound is so carefully curated, the weaker songs can feel like "gorging on candyfloss." Lushness that frames a strong melody can smother a slight one.

What is McDoom's own is the combination. Dub and folk have met before, but rarely on such intimate terms. Here the Jamaican side isn't a borrowed groove. It's her family, with her grandfather on the record and her father's records in the background, and the folk side is her own songwriting voice.
