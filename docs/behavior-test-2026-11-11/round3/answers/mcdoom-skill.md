# June McDoom, *Follow the Light* (Jagjaguwar, October 16, 2026)

*I haven't heard the record. This is built from McDoom's own interviews and statements and from critics who have heard it.*

## What it is

*Follow the Light* is the debut full-length from June McDoom, a New York-based singer and songwriter who grew up in a Jamaican family in South Florida. It has 12 songs: "Human Angel," "Without You," "Outside the Window," "Forever," "Get Out of My Life," "Grandpa," "Dream Dub," "Memory," "Mountain," "Searching to Find," "Weaver" and "Follow the Light." It follows two EPs: a self-titled one from 2022 and *With Strings* (2024), which brought in harp and string arrangements. Those EPs were collected in 2025, and the album is her first release on Jagjaguwar.

The short version is folk harmony laid over a dub rhythm section. Most of the songs start as voice and nylon-string guitar, the way a folk singer's would. Underneath, the bass is pushed to the front and the sound is soaked in echo, which is how a Jamaican dub engineer works. Critics also hear a third ingredient: lush, reverb-heavy "dream" production that pulls in shoegaze and 4AD comparisons. The album makes the most sense once you see where each of those three layers comes from, and why they sit together without clashing.

## Why folk and dub share the record: two musics in one house

McDoom describes her childhood home in one phrase: "two musics in one room." Her father, a carpenter with a serious interest in sound, built his own speakers and ran sound systems in separate rooms of the house. She would listen to folk records in her bedroom while his bass came up through the floor. Her grandfather, Colin Smith, is a Jamaican folk musician who has played guitar and banjo since the 1960s. He played guitar on a 1971 LP by the Jamaican Folk Singers, and the Jamaican government has honored him for keeping the music alive.

His music is **mento**, and it explains more of this album than the "folk" label does. Mento is Jamaica's rural folk music. It came before ska and reggae and fed into both. It is acoustic (banjo, guitar, hand drums), and the bass part is often played on a rumba box, a big thumb piano. So a Jamaican acoustic folk tradition already exists with a strong bass part and a dance rhythm underneath. When McDoom puts dub bass under fingerpicked songs, she isn't bolting two unrelated genres together. She is joining two branches of her own family's music: her grandfather's acoustic folk and her father's sound-system bass. She has said she wants to introduce listeners to styles like mento that they may not know.

She also arrived at Anglo-American folk separately. In high school she found Simon & Garfunkel through *The Graduate*, and she later trained as a jazz singer, earning a degree in jazz performance at The New School. She has said she loves folk but resists being called a "folk singer," which fits a record that only uses folk as one layer.

## How it was made: one part at a time, mostly by two people

The album was made slowly, at home, by McDoom and her longtime partner Evan Wright, a pianist who has worked with her since her first EP. Their method shapes the sound:

- **Voice and guitar first.** She writes on nylon-string guitar, melody first. Wright then fills in the harmonic parts under her top line, and he plays most of the instruments on the record.
- **Parts added one at a time, then thinned out.** McDoom calls herself "a super big part person": she builds a song from distinctive individual parts and adds them gradually. The sparseness people notice comes from that method. Because each layer is chosen on its own, few are wasted.
- **Her voice multiplied.** Most of the parts are vocal melodies she sings herself. She multitracks them into stacked harmonies. That's why the opener "Human Angel" sounds like voices overlapping in waves rather than a singer with backing vocals. The Line of Best Fit credits Wright's playing with freeing her to do this.
- **Miking an airy voice.** She says she has "a strange voice to capture because there's a lot of air in it," so she blends several microphones on each vocal. The breathiness is something they work to keep, not to hide.
- **A few outside hands.** Engineer Victor Axelrod recorded drum sounds in his Brooklyn basement. Preet Patel plays bass on "Dream Dub," and Kana Miyamoto plays traditional flute. A vintage wooden recorder she bought at a Brussels flea market appears on two tracks.

The production is detailed and deliberate, so it sounds lavish even though the band is small. The Line of Best Fit called the arrangements "unashamedly, unstintingly opulent," like an old Technicolor soundtrack.

## Why it sounds lush rather than rootsy: the arranging models

Folk and dub explain the ingredients. The way the songs are dressed comes from different sources, which McDoom names herself: Dionne Warwick, Minnie Riperton, Pentangle and Cocteau Twins. Three of these explain specific features:

- **Orchestral pop arranging.** She says Dionne Warwick's *Here I Am* was one of the first records that made her listen to how a record is arranged, and that Burt Bacharach's arrangements around Warwick's voice are still a major inspiration. That's the model for building a frame of instruments around a single voice instead of a band playing together. It explains why "Without You" can carry a harp, a dub bassline and big swells and still stay focused on the vocal. A critic likened that song to an '80s power ballad. Stereogum heard Harry Nilsson-style studio craft in it.
- **Layered, dreamy vocals.** McDoom mentions Cocteau Twins for their melodies and vocal layering, and she has pointed to *Victorialand*, their sparse 1986 album built on guitar with little drumming. That's the clearest precedent for her stacked vocal harmonies over thin, reverb-washed guitar. It's also where the "shoegaze" and "dream pop" descriptions come from.
- **Sacred-sounding contrast.** During recording she found Popol Vuh's *Hosianna Mantra* and it became a major reference. She admires its pure sound and its contrast of electric guitar with oboe and violin. The album does something similar when it sets a recorder or a traditional flute against dub bass and drums. Its hymn-like moments (one critic called "Without You" "hymn-like") come from this side too.

She also draws on classical music's dynamics, favoring sharp contrasts between heavy and intimate passages. That's why songs swell and then pull back instead of holding one level.

## The songs where the parts show most clearly

- **"Human Angel"** (opener): multitracked vocal waves. Stereogum heard a mix of acoustic balladry and "regal Kate Bush sophistipop" over a subtly driving, stuttering drum part.
- **"Without You"** (lead single): about realizing "how meaningless life can feel without the person/people you love." McDoom says she wanted "the arrangement to reflect the emotional journey of that realization," so the song builds as the realization does. The ending turns to a commitment never to take those people for granted, because "it's never too late to change how you've been."
- **"Get Out of My Life"**: a jaunty but fierce song about shaking off draining people. Near the end it shifts into a reggae rhythm with a recorder solo, the album's whole idea compressed into one song.
- **"Grandpa"**: her grandfather sings on it. Her father recorded his vocal in Florida and sent it to her, and it sits far beneath her voice in the mix. Three generations of the family appear on one track, with the oldest buried in the foundation.
- **"Dream Dub"** and the title track are where reggae comes most to the front: dub bass on the first, a gentle skank on the closer.

Lyrically, she reads widely for inspiration (she cites Zora Neale Hurston) and often uses nature as metaphor, which suits song titles like "Mountain" and "Weaver." Beyond the singles, little has been said about specific lyrics.

## Context and reception

McDoom has been building toward this record for several years. She toured with Nick Hakim and Brittany Howard and contributed to Sub Pop's 2024 Margo Guryan tribute, *Like Someone I Know*. Signing to Jagjaguwar put her debut on a well-known indie label. In interviews she pushes back on the pressure to treat herself as a brand and post constantly, and she encourages people "to make things they think are weird." The slow, homemade process is partly a choice in favor of that.

Early reviews have been warm. The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10 and called it "an enticing broadcast from an artist boldly operating in their own sphere." Its one complaint suggests where the record can fall short: on weaker songs, so much careful polish can feel like "gorging on candyfloss." That is the risk of making every song lush, and some listeners will find it too sweet. Clash described the album as moving from sleepy folk to "dubby low end."

## What is hers

None of the influences explains the core idea: dub's bass and space under folk singing, treated as one family inheritance rather than a genre experiment. Neither do the restraint of a two-person, part-by-part method or her airy, multiplied voice. The arranging models made the record lush. The house she grew up in decided what it is made of.
