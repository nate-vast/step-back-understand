mcdoom: B > A (slight)

**Preference:** B, slight.

**Main reason:** Both answers make the same central point, that folk sits on top of a dub/mento foundation inherited from her family. B gets there in about two-thirds of the reading time and fills in the background a newcomer is missing. Its clearest advantage is that it defines dub ("the 1970s Jamaican studio practice of remixing reggae tracks: stripping them down to drums and bass, dropping parts in and out, and drenching what remains in echo and reverb"). A builds its whole thesis on dub without ever saying what it is; the closest it gets is "the way a Jamaican dub engineer works." B also closes with a short listening path ("Dream Dub" and "Grandpa" back to back) that turns the explanation into something you can check with your ears. A explains more of *why* the record sounds lush, but that extra depth costs a lot of reading for a modest gain in understanding.

**Answer A**
- Strength: The explanation of causes is the richest of the two. Mento's rumba-box bass shows why folk and dub belong together, and the arranging models (Bacharach/Warwick, *Victorialand*, *Hosianna Mantra*) are each tied to a specific feature of the album.
- Weakness: It never defines dub, and it runs long. The tracklist is packed into a dense opening paragraph, and the "What is hers" ending repeats earlier points in rhetorical form.

**Answer B**
- Strength: It is tight and well ordered: a one-line key, how it works, why it sounds this way, then how to listen. It defines dub and mento in context, so a newcomer can follow every step.
- Weakness: It gives the arranging and dynamics side less depth (no Popol Vuh, no classical contrast), and some influences are only name-dropped.

**Flags**
- A, more about other things than the album: the arranging-models section leans toward describing the influences (Harry Nilsson-style craft, *Victorialand*'s 1986 drum-light sound, *Hosianna Mantra*'s oboe and violin) rather than the album. Most of it does link back to the album, though.
- A, padding: the "Context" paragraph on resisting being a brand and posting constantly, plus the Margo Guryan tribute and tour details, do little to explain the record. The "What is hers" conclusion restates what came before.
- A, list of facts: the tracklist, the EP history and the "A few outside hands" credits (Axelrod's basement, the Brussels flea-market recorder) are listed without explanation.
- B, list of facts without explanation: "Apple's notes put her alongside Judee Sill, Nina Simone and Alton Ellis." These are bare names. The tracklist and singles line in "What it is" is also bare data.
- B, padding: "As of mid-November 2026, Pitchfork, the Guardian and Paste had not reviewed it" tells you nothing about the album. The career recap (tours, Guryan tribute) also appears in B, though it is shorter there.
