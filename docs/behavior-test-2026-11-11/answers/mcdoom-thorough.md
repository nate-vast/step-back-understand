# June McDoom, *Follow the Light*

*Follow the Light* is June McDoom's debut full-length album, released 16 October 2026 on Jagjaguwar: 12 songs in just under 36 minutes. It lays 1960s-style folk harmony over Jamaican dub and reggae rhythm, the two kinds of music she grew up between. Her own summary of the aim was "trying to tie in the heritage that I've recently refound" with the rest of who she is.

## Where it comes from

McDoom (born Alyssa; "June" was a nickname of her grandmother Joan) grew up in a Jamaican household in South Florida, near the Everglades. Three family threads run through the record:

- **Her father**, a carpenter who built his own speakers and kept three sound systems in the house. The bass from his DJ sets came up through her bedroom floor while she listened to folk records.
- **Her grandfather, Colin Smith**, a mento musician (banjo and guitar) who worked to keep Jamaica's oldest rural folk style alive and gave her a guitar. He sings on "Grandpa".
- **Aggie**, described in the album notes as her grandmother and the centre of the family. "Memory" was written with her in mind.

She found folk on her own as a teenager through *The Sound of Music* and Simon & Garfunkel's songs in *The Graduate*; her parents called it "that sleepy music". She avoided Jamaican music at school to fit in, studied jazz performance at The New School in New York, and came back to it as an adult. The album notes describe her putting on an Alton Ellis record and realising the sound she wanted had been at home all along.

## How it was made

She made the album with Evan Wright, her partner of ten years, and the two co-produced it. Nearly every song began as her voice and a nylon-string guitar. She then sang each instrumental part and Wright played it, which is why the arrangements feel so melodic.

- **Drums** were recorded live with Victor Axelrod (Ticklah) in his Sunset Park basement, to get a 1960s reggae drum sound.
- **Everything else** was built in the couple's own studio in Brooklyn, on both tape and computer, with vintage gear such as an eight-track reel-to-reel and a Roland Space Echo. The harp was recorded at a friend's house.
- **Mixing** took twelve-hour days. She started the mix over after her parents told her, on a Christmas visit, that they couldn't hear enough of her voice.

Her stated reference points were Dionne Warwick, Minnie Riperton, Pentangle and Cocteau Twins' *Victorialand*, plus Popol Vuh's *Hosianna Mantra*. For the lyrics she took Zora Neale Hurston's plain nature imagery as a model. She told Rolling Stone she wanted the record "to feel kind of handmade even if it sounded not-perfect."

## What it is about

The title refers to her family. "The concept refers to how my family has shaped me in indirect ways," she told FLOOD: the light is the path lit by the people who came before her, and a choice to steer by them rather than by what drags her down. The title track began as an image of herself on a dark, rainy road, following a light.

Two other ideas recur. One is the many versions of a self, and what you owe the ones you have left behind ("Outside the Window"); the album notes put it as "Future You is an ancestor that your current self gets to meet." The other is not fitting in, which she felt at school, in jazz programmes and in folk, and which she now treats as something to be proud of.

## How it has been received

Reviews are positive, with one consistent reservation. I found no Pitchfork review.

| Outlet | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Qobuz | Named a Qobuzissime pick; "a masterpiece exceeding the expectations preceding it" |
| DIY | 4 stars; "stellar songcraft buoyed by endlessly inventive production" |
| Written in Music (NL) | Scored 4 (scale not shown); a "dream debut" |
| Song Bar | "Quietly distinctive and gorgeous debut" |
| The 13th Floor (NZ) | Unscored; praises its patience and restraint |
| The Line of Best Fit | 7/10; "an artist boldly operating in their own sphere" |

The reservation is that the lushness can blur. The Line of Best Fit wrote that when a song doesn't land, the record can feel like "gorging on candyfloss", and Song Bar found some arrangements so ornate that details compete, "Forever" in particular. The album also stays at one gentle dynamic level throughout, which is either its hypnotic strength or its limit, depending on the listener.

## Where to start

Play "Without You", "Dream Dub" and "Grandpa" back to back. They show the three poles of the album: the folk-meets-dub wall of sound, the dub side on its own, and the family history underneath. Then play the whole record in order, ideally on headphones or speakers with real bass, since the low end is half the idea.
