# June McDoom, *Follow the Light*

*Follow the Light* is June McDoom's debut album (Jagjaguwar, October 16, 2026; 12 songs, about 35 minutes). It is folk songwriting set on reggae and dub rhythm sections, with stacked, airy vocals and lush arrangements on top. The label's notes put it well: "not a reggae or dub record, not a folk record, but the harmony of one laid over the rhythm of the other."

I'm working from interviews, reviews and credits, not from listening, so the descriptions of the sound are reported ones.

## Why folk sits on top of dub

The album sounds like the house she grew up in. McDoom was raised in a Jamaican family in South Florida. Her father, a carpenter, built his own speakers and ran three sound systems at home, and the label notes describe her listening to folk records while bass came up through the floor. She found 1960s folk on her own, through *The Graduate* and Simon & Garfunkel.

That is why reviewers hear bass "front and centre" and echo that chops and twists the sound. Those are dub habits: the bass and drums are the foreground, and the mixing desk is played like an instrument. She told The Line of Best Fit she is tired of being filed as a folk singer and wants the album to "introduce people to the music that I'm really nerdy about."

## The drums

She cut live drums with Victor Axelrod (Ticklah) in his Brooklyn basement because he is "amazing at engineering drum sounds that sound like that '60s era of reggae drums." Axelrod comes from Antibalas and the Dap-Kings, co-produced *Dub Side of the Moon*, and produced the Frightnrs' rocksteady album for Daptone. His specialty is new recordings that sound like old ones. So expect drums closer to an old rocksteady 45 than to a modern indie-folk kit; that is my inference from who recorded them.

Stereogum's writers heard Kate Bush in the drum parts on "Human Angel" and "Get Out Of My Life." That comparison is theirs, not hers.

## How the parts are made

She writes on nylon-string guitar, then composes every other part by singing it: "they're all vocal melodies," bass lines included. Her partner Evan Wright, who plays most of the instruments, turns them into parts, and the two can spend hours on one bassline. This is why every layer is described as melodic, and it is the most distinctive thing about the record. She studied jazz at The New School and says it made her "a really technical creator," which fits the dense harmony stacks.

She names Cocteau Twins for vocal layering. She also found Popol Vuh's *Hosianna Mantra* while recording and calls it "a big reference record" for how "pure" it sounds. That 1972 album is nearly all acoustic (piano, oboe, guitar, soprano) and was conceived as a kind of mass. It may explain the quality one reviewer called "a hymn from another realm," though she hasn't drawn that line herself.

## The lush arrangements

The record has harp, strings, flute and a flea-market wooden recorder she plays herself. Her model is Dionne Warwick's *Here I Am*, the record that made her love producing: "Burt Bacharach sets up all the sounds around Dionne in the most special way." The orchestration is meant to frame a voice. Getting that balance was hard: she restarted the mix after her parents said they couldn't hear enough of her vocals.

## Family as the subject

"Grandpa" (1:09) is a duet with her grandfather, Colin Smith, built on a phone voice memo her father recorded in Florida. The two never played together. Smith was part of the Jamaican Folk Singers, a choir that arranged rural songs (mento, work songs, Revival and Kumina songs) for the concert stage. She says he plays guitar on their 1971 album; one discography I found lists a Colin Smith among its bass singers. Either way, her taste for harmony has a Jamaican folk root as well as an American one. That is my reading, not her statement.

"Memory" was written for her grandmother Aggie. "Without You" is about how empty life feels without the people you love. She says she was far more deliberate about lyrics than on her EPs.

## Reception

Coverage is still thin. The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10: "an enticing broadcast from an artist boldly operating in their own sphere," but like "gorging on candyfloss" when the songs are weaker. Clash called it excellent.

## Where to start

"Without You" (harp over a dub bassline), "Dream Dub" and the title track (the most openly reggae), then "Grandpa."
