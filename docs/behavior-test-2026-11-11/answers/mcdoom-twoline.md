# June McDoom, *Follow the Light*

*Follow the Light* is the debut full-length by June McDoom, a New York singer-songwriter who grew up in a Jamaican household in South Florida. Jagjaguwar released it on October 16, 2026: twelve songs in just under 36 minutes. The whole record runs on one idea, which the label's notes state as "not a reggae or dub record, not a folk record, but the harmony of one laid over the rhythm of the other."

## How the songs are built

Nearly every song started as McDoom's voice and a nylon-string guitar. The rest was added one part at a time in the home studio she shares with her partner and longtime collaborator, Evan Wright, who plays most of the instruments.

The method explains most of what you hear. McDoom sings each part she wants, including the basslines, and Wright works out how to play it. "That's how we make all the parts of the track, they're all vocal melodies," she told The Line of Best Fit. So the bass carries tunes of its own, and it sits forward in the mix the way it does on a dub record, while her stacked vocal harmonies take the space above it. Harp, strings, piano and recorder fill the middle, and dub studio effects (spring reverb, echo, sirens) are applied to folk material.

Three production choices shape the result:

- **Drums.** Most of the folk records McDoom loves have none, and she finds drums hard to fit beside delicate guitars. For a couple of songs she went to Victor Axelrod, the Brooklyn reggae producer who records as Ticklah, because he can get the drum sound of 1960s Jamaican records. Those were cut in his basement, and everything else was done at home on tape and digital, apart from the harp, recorded at a friend's house.
- **Voice.** She describes her voice as having "a lot of air in it," which no single microphone captures, so she blends several.
- **Mix.** She mixed for twelve hours a day and then started over after her parents, hearing it at Christmas, said they could not hear enough of her vocals.

She set out to keep the record minimal. Strings and harp went in only where a song needed more depth, which is why sparse tracks sit next to lavish ones. She traces that contrast to classical music's swings between heavy passages and a single quiet instrument.

## Why it sounds this way

The combination comes from her childhood bedroom. Her father, a carpenter, built his own speakers and ran three sound systems in the house, and the bass from his DJ sets came up through her floor while she listened to folk records she had found on her own through *The Graduate* and Simon & Garfunkel. Her parents called it "that sleepy music." Nobody around her listened to it, and she says she fell for harmony before words. The album puts those two sounds back in one room.

Her grandfather, Colin Smith, is the other source. He plays mento, the rural acoustic Jamaican folk music that came before ska, rocksteady and reggae, and he has spent decades keeping it alive. He steered her toward the guitar, so the folk side of the record also has a Jamaican root.

Two further things account for the finish. She trained as a jazz singer at The New School, and says it left her a "really technical creator." And she treats the sound of a recording as an instrument in itself, an interest she dates to hearing how Burt Bacharach arranged the sounds around Dionne Warwick.

## What it is about

McDoom says many of the songs are about family and relationships "that feel so pure." She grew up among dozens of relatives who had each emigrated from Jamaica and regrouped in South Florida, and she felt out of place at school, so family was where she belonged. The record's second subject is keeping hold of yourself as an adult. "Outside the Window" and "Searching to Find" both ask how much of a childhood self survives.

The title refers to guidance from people who love you. The label's notes place her grandmother Aggie at the centre of the record and describe that light as still present "even when their form has moved on." The same notes frame the album as the half-awake state at the edge of sleep, which fits its slow tempos and blurred edges.

The words are plain on purpose. McDoom was reading Zora Neale Hurston while writing and wanted Hurston's way of using nature to say something difficult simply.

## Reception

Reviews were favourable. The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10 and called its dub-reggae-folk hybrids the work of "an artist boldly operating in their own sphere." DIY gave it four stars, and Qobuz made it a Qobuzissime editor's pick. The recurring reservation is richness: where a song is slighter, The Line of Best Fit found the lavish arrangements "slightly resemble gorging on candyfloss," and Song Bar heard details competing for attention.

McDoom rejects the folk label that follows her around. "I sing and play guitar," she says, and she describes the album as a way to introduce listeners to Jamaican music she is "really nerdy about" by adding small elements of it. Her parents, who never liked folk, like this record because they hear the reggae in it.
