1. The recipe is the harmony of folk laid over the rhythm of reggae and dub. Jamaican music supplies the bottom: live drums recorded to sound like 1960s reggae, and dub basslines pushed to the front of the mix with echo. Folk supplies the top: nylon-string guitar and stacked, airy vocal harmonies. Other specific sounds include harp, strings, flute, a wooden recorder and shoegaze-like swells.

2. A song starts as McDoom's voice and nylon-string guitar, and at that stage she does not think about other instruments. For this album she says she wanted to be intentional about everything she was saying in the lyrics, and she first aimed for more minimal arrangements, adding only where she wanted depth; I am not certain which of these the question means by "rule." She then sings each part she wants, bass lines included, and Evan Wright, a pianist who plays most of the instruments, works them out, sometimes over hours on a single bassline. The instrumental parts feel sung because they began as vocal melodies.

3. The live drums were cut with Victor Axelrod (Ticklah) in his basement in Sunset Park, Brooklyn, chosen because he is good at getting 1960s-era reggae drum sounds. Most of the rest was tracked by McDoom and Wright in their home studio, to both tape and digital, with the harp recorded at a friend's house. She says her voice is hard to capture because it has a lot of air in it, so she uses several microphones and treats recording like a puzzle. During mixing, her parents said they couldn't hear enough of her vocals, and she restarted the mix.

4. The Jamaican half comes from home: she grew up in a Jamaican household in South Florida where her father, a carpenter, built his own speakers and ran three sound systems, and her grandfather Colin Smith was a mento and folk musician who put a guitar in her hands. The folk half she found on her own, through The Graduate and Simon & Garfunkel, followed by jazz study in New York. She has said she is tired of being boxed in as a folk singer and wants the album to introduce people to the Jamaican music she is nerdy about. The label notes mention a moment when she put on an Alton Ellis record, but I do not know the details of that story.

5. I do not know for certain. The label notes do not define the title directly; the closest line is that we turn into the light when things feel dark, and they place her grandmother Aggie at the core of the record, with "Memory" written for her. The songs are broadly about family and close relationships, and about the different versions of oneself. I do not know when McDoom says she realized what the songs were about.

6. Before the album she released a self-titled EP in 2022 and the With Strings EP, both made at home with Evan Wright, which were later collected on a self-titled compilation. She signed to Jagjaguwar, announced the album in early August 2026 with the single "Without You," and released it on October 16, 2026. The album adds live drums, dub bass and overt reggae rhythm, real harp and string arrangements building on With Strings, more deliberate lyrics, and a duet with her grandfather.

7. Reception was positive, though I saw only a few reviews. Writers praised the dreamy, layered vocals, the lavish arrangements and the way hazy psych-folk melts into dub and reggae; The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10 and called it an enticing broadcast from an artist in her own sphere, and Clash called it excellent. The main reservation was that when the songs themselves are less compelling, the richness can feel like gorging on candyfloss.

8. This is a prediction, not something I have seen reported. The songs should sound like what they were at the start, since each began as voice and nylon-string guitar: quiet, close folk songs carried by the melody and her airy voice. Missing would be the whole bottom half and the production: drums, dub bass, echo, the stacked harmonies, harp and strings, so the reggae side would mostly disappear unless she implies it in her guitar rhythm. They still work because the writing came before the arrangement. I would guess she has mixed feelings: she calls herself a "parts" person and very technical, so she would miss the layers, but I do not know what she has said about playing solo.
