1. The recipe is the harmony of folk laid over the rhythm of dub and reggae. Jamaican music supplies the bottom (dub basslines, reggae and rocksteady drum feel, echo and reverb), and 1960s-style folk supplies the top (nylon-string guitar and stacked, airy vocal harmonies). Specific sounds include a harp rippling over a deep dub bassline on "Without You", dub sirens and Space Echo-style delay on "Dream Dub", flea-market recorder, flute and string quartet, and shoegaze-like guitar shimmer in the vein of Cocteau Twins.

2. Nearly every song starts as just McDoom's voice and a nylon-string guitar, with no thought yet for other instruments. This time she set herself a strict rule to be intentional about the lyrics first, before any of the production work, aiming for simple words and nature imagery modelled on Zora Neale Hurston. She and Wright then build the arrangement one instrument at a time over a long period: she sings each part she hears (a bassline, for example) and he plays it, since he plays most of the instruments, while she layers the vocal harmonies. Because every instrumental line began as a melody she sang, the parts keep a vocal shape, which is why the record is so melody-heavy.

3. The live drums were cut first with Victor Axelrod (Ticklah) in his Sunset Park, Brooklyn basement, to get a 1960s reggae drum sound. The rest was built by McDoom and Wright in their own Brooklyn studio on both tape and computer, using gear such as an eight-track reel-to-reel, Ampex preamps, a Roland Space Echo and a Jupiter-8, with the harp recorded at a friend's house. Her voice has a lot of air in it and is hard to capture, so she records it through several different microphones and pieces the results together "like a puzzle". She mixed for twelve hours a day and started the mix over after her parents said, on a Christmas visit, that they couldn't hear enough of her vocals; the listed credits have Al Carlson mixing most tracks, the couple mixing the rest, and Noel Summerville mastering.

4. The Jamaican half is her family: she grew up in a Jamaican household in South Florida where her father, a carpenter, built his own speakers and ran several sound systems, and her grandfather Colin Smith was a mento musician who gave her a guitar. The folk half she found alone as a teenager, through *The Sound of Music* and Simon & Garfunkel in *The Graduate*, while her parents called it "that sleepy music". She pushed the Jamaican music away at school to fit in and only came back to it as an adult, when an Alton Ellis record made her realise the sound she was after had been there her whole life. She says the purpose of the record was to tie that refound heritage to the other sides of her identity, and to make the music she wished had existed when she was young.

5. The light is her family, the people who came before her and shaped her: she told FLOOD the concept "refers to how my family has shaped me in indirect ways", and the album notes single out her grandmother Aggie as the guide at its centre. It is also a choice to focus on the positive things that give direction instead of the things that drag her down, and the title song began as an image of herself on a dark, rainy road following a light. She says she is trying to find herself through the music by including the different parts of herself, and the notes describe her working out who she is in real time as she layers the songs. I don't know of a specific moment she named for when she realised what the songs were about, so I can't answer that part.

6. Before the album she released a self-titled EP in 2022, made at home with Evan Wright and issued physically by Temporary Residence in 2023 (it includes her first single, "The City"), and the *With Strings* EP in 2024, which added harp and string arrangements. She then signed to Jagjaguwar, announced the album on 4 August 2026 with "Without You", followed with "Get Out of My Life" and "Human Angel", and released it on 16 October 2026. What the album adds to her hushed folk is the Jamaican low end: dub bass, reggae drums, echo, and her grandfather's voice, along with fuller, more layered arrangements.

7. The reception was positive: Qobuz made it a Qobuzissime pick and called it a masterpiece, DIY gave it four stars, and The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10. Reviewers consistently praised how natural and original the folk-dub blend sounds, the layered vocal harmonies and detailed arrangements, its patience and restraint, and the family story, with "Grandpa" often singled out. The main reservation was that the lushness can be too much: The Line of Best Fit compared the weaker moments to "gorging on candyfloss", and Song Bar felt some arrangements, "Forever" especially, were so ornate that details competed with the song.

8. Stripped to one voice and one guitar, the songs would revert to how they were written, as spare folk songs with clear melodies, closer to her early hushed work. What goes missing is everything the album adds afterwards: the dub bass and drums, the echo and reverb, the harp and strings, and above all the stacked harmonies, so the Jamaican half of the recipe would be heard only as a hint in her strumming. They still work because each one began as voice and guitar, with lyrics and melody settled before any production. This is a prediction, not something I have an account of: I would expect her to be comfortable with the bareness and perhaps to enjoy the intimacy, but to miss the parts and the bass, since she calls herself a "parts person" and treats the recording as an instrument in the song.
