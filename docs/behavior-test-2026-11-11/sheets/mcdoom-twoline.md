1. The recipe is folk harmony laid over reggae and dub rhythm. Jamaican music supplies the bottom: tuneful basslines pushed forward in the mix, reggae-style drums, and dub effects such as spring reverb, echo and sirens. Folk supplies the top: fingerpicked nylon-string guitar and McDoom's stacked vocal harmonies, with harp, strings and a wooden recorder in between.

2. Nearly every song starts as just her voice and a nylon-string guitar, with no thought yet for other instruments. Her rule this time was to keep things minimal, limiting the instrumentation and adding strings or harp only where a song needed more depth; she also set out to be more deliberate about the lyrics than on her first EP. She and Evan Wright then build the arrangement one part at a time, sometimes spending a whole day on a bassline: she sings the part she wants and he works out how to play it. Because every part, even the bass, begins as a melody she sang, the instrumental lines move like vocal lines.

3. Live drums for a couple of songs were cut with Victor Axelrod (Ticklah) in his basement in Sunset Park, Brooklyn, because he is good at the drum sound of 1960s reggae records. Everything else was built by McDoom and Wright in their home studio, tracking to both tape and digital, except the harp, which was recorded at a friend's house. She says her voice has a lot of air in it that no single microphone captures, so she blends several mics. She mixed for twelve hours a day and started the mix over after her parents, on a Christmas visit, said they couldn't hear enough of her vocals.

4. The Jamaican half is her family: her father, a carpenter, built his own speakers and ran three sound systems at home, and his bass came up through her bedroom floor, while her grandfather Colin Smith is a mento musician who steered her to the guitar. The folk half she found alone, through The Graduate and Simon & Garfunkel, while her parents called it "that sleepy music," and she fell for harmony before words. For years the Jamaican music felt like her family's sound and not her own; the label notes say she put on an Alton Ellis record one day and realized the sound she had been seeking had been there all along. She has also said she wants to introduce listeners to Jamaican music she is nerdy about by adding small elements of it.

5. The light is guidance from people who love you, and the label notes place her grandmother Aggie at the centre of it, describing that energy as still present even after the person is gone; "Memory" was written with her in mind. McDoom says the songs are largely about family and relationships that feel pure, and that her songs always seem to end up being about finding yourself and holding on to something childlike as an adult. I do not know a specific moment when she says she realized this; the closest I have is her remark that she has realized as an adult how much her family shaped her.

6. Before this she released the single "The City" and a self-titled EP in 2022, home-recorded with Wright, then the With Strings EP in 2024, which brought in harp and string arrangements. Follow the Light is her first release on Jagjaguwar: the signing and the album were announced on August 4, 2026 with "Without You," followed by "Get Out of My Life" and "Human Angel," and the album came out on October 16, 2026. What it adds is the Jamaican low end (dub basslines, reggae rhythms, drums and dub effects) under the folk and harp sound of the EPs, plus more carefully worked lyrics.

7. Reception was favourable: The Line of Best Fit gave it 7/10, DIY four stars, and Qobuz made it an editor's pick. Reviewers consistently praised how natural the folk-and-dub hybrid sounds, along with the vocal harmonies, the sense of space and the meticulous production. The main reservation was richness: where a song is slighter the lavish arrangements can cloy (The Line of Best Fit compared it to gorging on candyfloss), and Song Bar heard details competing for attention.

8. This is a prediction, since I do not know how such a set actually went or what she said about it. Alone with a guitar the songs would fall back to their starting form, sounding like quiet, harmony-rich folk songs, and most of the Jamaican half would be missing: the forward bass, the drums, the dub echo, the harp and the stacked harmonies. They would still work because each one was written as voice and guitar first, with the melody and chords carrying the song before any arrangement existed. I would expect her to feel the loss, since she calls herself a "parts person" who tries to play every small part exactly live and treats the sound of a recording as an instrument in itself, though she might also see it as the songs in their original state.
